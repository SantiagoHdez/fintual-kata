  Decision 3: Numeric type for money and shares

  The rebalance math divides and multiplies prices, weights and quantities. The
  type we pick decides whether a 40/60 split ends up exact or off by a fraction
  of a cent.

  There are two things to type: money (prices, portfolio value, trade amounts)
  and quantity (number of shares). They don't have to share a type.

  Money

  Option: decimal
  Advantages: Base-10 exact for values like 0.1 and 19.99. Built into C#, and
  the
  idiomatic choice for money.
  Tradeoffs: Slower than double, which doesn't matter here. It still can't
  represent 1/3 exactly, so we need an explicit rounding policy.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: double
  Advantages: Fast and familiar.
  Tradeoffs: Binary floating point drifts: 0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3. Weights summing to
  100% could fail an equality check. Reviewers would flag it.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Integer minor units (cents as long)
  Advantages: Exact and fast.
  Tradeoffs: Awkward for stock prices that go to 4 decimals, and for fractional
  quantities. Weights still need division and rounding.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: A Money value type wrapping decimal
  Advantages: Prevents mixing currencies or units, and makes signatures
  self-documenting.
  Tradeoffs: Extra code for a small kata. The task never mentions currencies.

  Recommendation: plain decimal for money. A Money type would be
  over-engineering here, and we can mention it as a possible extension in the
  docs.

  Quantity (shares)

  This depends on D5 (fractional vs whole shares), which comes next, so I'd tie
  the two together:
  - If fractional shares are allowed, quantity should also be decimal.
  - If only whole shares are allowed, int or long is the better fit, and the
    leftover cash has to be handled.

  I'd decide the quantity type after D5 and settle money now.

  Allocation weights

  The target allocation (40%, 60%) could be stored as a decimal fraction (0.40)

  Decision 5 (remainder): Rounding policy for quantities

  Fractional shares are allowed, so there is no leftover cash from whole-share
  rounding. But decimal can't represent a repeating value like 1/3 exactly. A
  trade of $100 / $30 per share gives 3.3333…, so we need to decide how precise
  a quantity is.

  5a: Precision of share quantities

  Option: Don't round (keep full decimal precision, ~28 digits)
  Advantages: Simplest code. No rounding bias.
  Tradeoffs: Orders with absurd precision like 3.3333333333333333333333333333
  shares. Not realistic for a brokerage, and the trades won't re-sum exactly.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Round to N decimals (e.g. 4, or 6)
  Advantages: Realistic output. Brokers do cap fractional precision.
  Tradeoffs: N is a business choice we have to justify. Rounding means a tiny
  residual mismatch against the target.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Make precision a configurable parameter (default e.g. 4)
  Advantages: Flexible, and shows the assumption explicitly.
  Tradeoffs: A bit more API surface.

  5b: Rounding mode

  Mode: ToZero (truncate)
  Behaviour: Never overshoots. Buys never exceed available funds, and sells
  never
  exceed holdings.
  Tradeoffs: Slight systematic under-trading.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Mode: AwayFromZero / ToEven (banker's)
  Behaviour: Closest to the true value, unbiased in aggregate (banker's).
  Tradeoffs: Can overshoot by a hair. A sell could marginally exceed holdings,
  or
  total buys exceed total sell proceeds.

  Recommendation: make precision a constructor or method parameter with a
  default of 4 decimal places, and round with ToZero. Under a self-financing
  rebalance (D6), safety matters more than perfect accuracy. A sell can never
  exceed holdings, and buys never exceed the cash raised by the sells.

  5c: Where does rounding happen?

  - Only on the final order quantities. All intermediate math (total value,
    target values, deltas) stays at full decimal precision. This avoids
    compounding errors.
  - At every step. Rarely sensible.

  Recommendation: round only the final order quantities.

  ---

  What I need from you:
  1. 5a: no rounding, fixed N, or configurable? If N, which value?
  2. 5b: ToZero or ToEven or AwayFromZero?
  3. 5c: round only at the end?

  Decision 6: Cash and self-financing

  This decides what "total portfolio value" means and where the money for buys
  comes from.

  6a: Does the portfolio hold cash?

  Option: No cash. Value = sum of holdings × price
  Advantages: Simplest. Matches the task, which only mentions stocks and
  allocation.
  Tradeoffs: Can't model deposits, or leftover proceeds. A real app has cash.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Portfolio has a cash balance, and cash is part of total value
  Advantages: Realistic. A user can deposit money and rebalance to deploy it.
  Tradeoffs: Raises the question of whether cash is an allocation target. If the

  allocation sums to 100% across stocks, cash would be driven to 0.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Cash balance exists but is excluded from the rebalance target
  Advantages: Keeps cash as a buffer.
  Tradeoffs: Two notions of value (invested vs total). More complex to explain.

  6b: Must the rebalance be self-financing?

  Option: Self-financing: buys are funded only by sells
  Advantages: Fits the "no cash" model. Total value is conserved: sell proceeds
  equal buy cost. Easy to verify in tests.
  Tradeoffs: Can't deploy new money.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Can use available cash for buys
  Advantages: Lets a user deposit and invest.
  Tradeoffs: Requires 6a with cash, and a rule for what happens when cash is
  insufficient.

  Interaction with D5

  With 4-decimal banker's rounding, sell proceeds and buy cost may differ by a
  sliver. With no cash balance, that residual has nowhere to go, so tests need a
  tolerance. With a cash balance, the residual is just held as cash.

  Recommendation: no cash, self-financing. The task describes only stocks and a
  target distribution, so this is the minimal model that answers the question.
  The target value of each stock is then weight × total holdings value, and the
  sells and buys net to zero, up to rounding. Then I'd note in the docs that
  adding cash is a natural extension.

  The case for cash is realism: someone depositing money into a portfolio is the
  most common reason to rebalance in practice. It would cost one extra field
  and a rule about whether cash counts as part of total value.

  Decision 7: Rebalance output and mutation

  Holdings are mutable (D2c). The question is what Rebalance does with that.

  7a: Does Rebalance apply the trades or only report them?

  Option: A. Pure: returns the orders and leaves the portfolio untouched
  Advantages: Matches the task wording ("know which stocks should be sold and
  bought"). Safe to call repeatedly. Easy to test, and nothing happens until
  the caller confirms.
  Tradeoffs: The caller must apply the orders. The portfolio's Buy/Sell methods
  (D2c) do that, so you need a second call.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: B. Applies the trades itself and returns what it did
  Advantages: One call does everything.
  Tradeoffs: Surprising side effects. A "what should I do?" question changes
  state. Hard to preview, and risky if a price moves or a trade would fail in
  real life.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: C. Both: a pure Rebalance() plus an ApplyRebalance() (or 
  Apply(orders))
  Advantages: Preview and execute are explicit and separate.
  Tradeoffs: More API surface than the task asks for.

  In a real trading app, executing a trade involves a broker, so an app would
  want to review the plan before executing it. That favors A.

  Recommendation: A. It answers exactly what the task asks. Mutability stays
  available through Buy/Sell, so the caller can apply the plan. I'd add C's
  Apply only if you want a demo of the full loop, which would be extra surface.

  7b: Shape of the result

  Option: Flat list of Order(Stock, Side, Quantity), Side being Buy or Sell,
  with
  quantity always positive
  Advantages: Simple to iterate and print. Positive quantities avoid sign
  confusion.
  Tradeoffs: The caller filters if they want only sells or only buys.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Result object with Sells and Buys lists
  Advantages: Mirrors the task wording ("which should be sold and which
  bought").
  Tradeoffs: Two lists to maintain.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: Signed quantity per stock (+ buy, - sell)
  Advantages: Compact.
  Tradeoffs: Easy to misread, and less self-documenting.

  Recommendation: a flat list of orders using a Side enum and positive
  quantities, with sells listed before buys. Each order is an immutable record.
  The ordering conveys the natural execution sequence for a self-financing
  rebalance (raise cash first, then spend it).

  7c: Which stocks appear in the result?

  - Every stock, including zero-quantity orders. Noisy.

  Decision 9: Drift tolerance and minimum trade size

  Right now Rebalance would emit an order for any stock whose rounded delta
  isn't 0. A portfolio that has drifted 0.01% from target would still produce
  trades. That's technically correct, but real brokers charge per trade, and
  tiny trades aren't worth it.

  Option: A. No threshold: trade whenever the rounded delta ≠ 0
  Advantages: Simplest. Always lands on target (up to rounding). Nothing extra
  to
  explain or test.
  Tradeoffs: Noisy for tiny drift. The only filtering is the 4-decimal rounding
  from D5.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: B. Drift tolerance (e.g. skip if a stock is within ±1 percentage point
   
  of target)
  Advantages: Avoids pointless trades, and is a common feature of real
  rebalancing.
  Tradeoffs: A business-policy choice to justify. The portfolio ends up slightly

  off target on purpose. Adds a parameter and test cases. Interacts with
  self-financing: skipping some trades breaks the sells-fund-buys balance
  unless handled carefully.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: C. Minimum trade value (e.g. skip orders worth less than $1)
  Advantages: Easy to explain, since it is about the trade, not the portfolio.
  Tradeoffs: Same self-financing complication as B. The threshold is arbitrary.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Option: D. Optional threshold parameter that defaults to "none"
  Advantages: Keeps A's behavior by default and leaves room for B/C.
  Tradeoffs: Still builds a feature the task doesn't ask for. A YAGNI violation,

  which you asked to respect.

  The self-financing point matters. If you skip a small sell but still make the
  matching buy, the buy is no longer funded by sells. You would need to scale
  the other trades or drop the buy too, which is a design problem of its own.

  Recommendation: A, with no threshold. The task says to have a balanced
  portfolio based on the allocation, and a threshold is a policy layer on top of
  that. It can be listed in the docs as a future extension, along with the
  self-financing caveat. This is consistent with how we handled cash in D6.

  How Rebalance() works

  1. It throws InvalidOperationException if no allocation is set.
  2. It reads each price once for every stock held or allocated, and works from
     that snapshot. TotalValue() uses the same snapshot code.
  3. For each stock, the target shares are total × weight ÷ price, and the delta
     against the shares held becomes an order. A held stock that isn't
     allocated has weight 0 and is sold entirely. An allocated stock that isn't
     held is bought.
  4. The final quantity is rounded to 4 decimals with banker's rounding. A sell
     is capped at the shares held. An order that rounds to 0 is dropped.
  5. The result lists sells first, then buys, each sorted by symbol. It never
     changes the portfolio.

  The spec example checks out. A 10 META @500 and 5 AAPL @200 portfolio with a
  40/60 target gives sell 5.2 META and buy 13 AAPL, and both sides come to 2600.
